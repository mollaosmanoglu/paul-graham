### Schedule

<Note kind="essay" source="Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule" href="http://www.paulgraham.com/makersschedule.html" date="2009-07">
There are two types of schedule, which I'll call the manager's schedule and the maker's schedule.
</Note>

<Note kind="essay" source="Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule" href="http://www.paulgraham.com/makersschedule.html" date="2009-07">
You can't write or program well in units of an hour.
</Note>

<Note kind="essay" source="Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule" href="http://www.paulgraham.com/makersschedule.html" date="2009-07">
You can block off several hours for a single task if you need to, but by default you change what you're doing every hour.
</Note>

<Note kind="essay" source="Good and Bad Procrastination" href="http://www.paulgraham.com/procrastination.html" date="2005-12">
The reason it pays to put off even those errands is that real work needs two things errands don't: big chunks of time, and the right mood.
</Note>

<Note kind="essay" source="Holding a Program in One's Head" href="http://www.paulgraham.com/head.html" date="2007-08">
Don't have multiple people editing the same piece of code.
</Note>

<Note kind="essay" source="Holding a Program in One's Head" href="http://www.paulgraham.com/head.html" date="2007-08">
You can start to treat parts as black boxes once you feel confident you've fully explored them.
</Note>

<Note kind="essay" source="Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule" href="http://www.paulgraham.com/makersschedule.html" date="2009-07">
Don't your spirits rise at the thought of having an entire day free to work, with no appointments at all?
</Note>

<Note kind="essay" source="Good and Bad Procrastination" href="http://www.paulgraham.com/procrastination.html" date="2005-12">
You can tighten the angle once you get going, just as a sailboat can sail closer to the wind once it gets underway.
</Note>

<Note kind="essay" source="Good and Bad Procrastination" href="http://www.paulgraham.com/procrastination.html" date="2005-12">
You have to work on small things that could grow into big things, or work on successively larger things, or split the moral load with collaborators.
</Note>

<Note kind="essay" source="Holding a Program in One's Head" href="http://www.paulgraham.com/head.html" date="2007-08">
You don't even let yourself think of such things.
</Note>

<Note kind="essay" source="Good and Bad Procrastination" href="http://www.paulgraham.com/procrastination.html" date="2005-12">
You can't look a big problem too directly in the eye.
</Note>

<Note kind="essay" source="Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule" href="http://www.paulgraham.com/makersschedule.html" date="2009-07">

<Note kind="essay" source="The Top Idea in Your Mind" href="http://www.paulgraham.com/top.html" date="2010-07">

### Meetings

<Note kind="essay" source="Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule" href="http://www.paulgraham.com/makersschedule.html" date="2009-07">
When you're operating on the maker's schedule, meetings are a disaster.
</Note>

<Note kind="essay" source="Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule" href="http://www.paulgraham.com/makersschedule.html" date="2009-07">
A single meeting can blow a whole afternoon, by breaking it into two pieces each too small to do anything hard in.
</Note>

<Note kind="essay" source="Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule" href="http://www.paulgraham.com/makersschedule.html" date="2009-07">
For someone on the maker's schedule, having a meeting is like throwing an exception.
</Note>

<Note kind="tweet" source="@paulg" href="https://x.com/paulg/status/1496166445549563908" date="2022-02">
Meetings are a necessary evil. Necessary, but still evil. So there should be as few as possible, and they should be as short as possible.
</Note>
